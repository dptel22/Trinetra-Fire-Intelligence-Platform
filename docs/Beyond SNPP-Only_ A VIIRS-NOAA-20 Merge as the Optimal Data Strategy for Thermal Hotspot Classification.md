# Beyond SNPP-Only: A VIIRS-NOAA-20 Merge as the Optimal Data Strategy for Thermal Hotspot Classification

## Comparative Analysis of Five FIRMS Satellite Platforms

The selection of an optimal satellite sensor for a machine learning classification task is contingent upon a nuanced evaluation of its technical specifications, data characteristics, and practical implications within the project's unique constraints. The user's goal requires prioritizing coverage continuity over India during August 2025–August 2026 to support the computation of a critical temporal feature, `persistence_90d`, while ensuring consistent feature representation across all six classes, including 'unclassified' [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528)]. This analysis examines each of the five available FIRMS platforms—VIIRS/SNPP, VIIRS/NOAA-20, VIIRS/NOAA-21, MODIS/Terra, and MODIS/Aqua—through the lens of these priorities, distinguishing between documented evidence from NASA literature, peer-reviewed studies, and reasoned inference based on available data. The central strategic constraint is that any solution must be viable within the tight timeframe of a hackathon, favoring pragmatic engineering decisions that yield proportional gains in model performance and data reliability [[55](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/382153/2/382153.pdf)].

The first platform, VIIRS/Suomi-NPP (SNPP), serves as the baseline for the existing dataset. Its primary strength lies in its long operational history, having been collecting data since January 20, 2012, which provides a deep temporal context for feature engineering [[31](https://firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/download/)]. As the source of the current one-year dataset, it is already integrated into the pipeline. Its key advantage is its high spatial resolution of 375 meters for I-band active fire detections, superior to MODIS and enabling the capture of smaller, more isolated thermal events [[6](https://pyronear.org/pyro-risks/overview/datasets/NASA-FIRMS_ACTIVE-FIRE_VIIRS.html), [30](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/instruments/viirs/land-near-real-time-data)]. Numerous peer-reviewed studies have successfully utilized SNPP VIIRS data for fire monitoring, establishing its credibility and reliability in the scientific community [[21](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/centers/lp-daac/publications?combine), [23](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/topics/human-dimensions/natural-hazards/publications?combine)]. However, its single-platform nature presents a significant vulnerability. The user has identified 11 missing days of coverage within the specified one-year window, a direct threat to the integrity of the `persistence_90d` feature [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528)]. This single, critical flaw disqualifies SNPP as a sufficient standalone source for the project's objectives, despite its established utility and high-resolution data.

The second platform, VIIRS/NOAA-20, emerges as the most compelling candidate to augment the SNPP data. It shares the exact same instrument design and 375-meter I-band spatial resolution as SNPP, meaning that merging data from both sources introduces no loss of spatial fidelity or feature detail [[30](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/instruments/viirs/land-near-real-time-data)]. Launched in April 2017, NOAA-20 extends the timeline of available high-resolution data significantly [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10869837/), [31](https://firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/download/)]. Critically, NOAA-20 operates in a near-tandem orbit with SNPP, flying approximately 50 minutes behind it [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528)]. This configuration effectively halves the revisit time over any given location on Earth, dramatically increasing the probability of capturing transient thermal events and, most importantly, filling the data gaps inherent to a single satellite's orbital track. This tandem operation is a powerful mechanism for enhancing coverage continuity, directly addressing the primary weakness of the SNPP-only dataset. While its launch date is later than SNPP's, its full operational status makes it an ideal partner to create a robust, gap-free dataset for the entire evaluation period.

The third platform, VIIRS/NOAA-21, further extends the VIIRS constellation but offers diminishing returns compared to NOAA-20. Like its predecessors, it maintains the 375-meter resolution and contributes to the overall data volume [[30](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/instruments/viirs/land-near-real-time-data)]. NOAA-21 was launched in November 2022 and began collecting science data in December 2022, making it fully operational for the 2025-2026 period [[31](https://firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/download/), [35](https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/news/first-image-released-noaa-21-viirs-instrument)]. Its position in the tandem orbit, flying another 50 minutes behind NOAA-20, pushes the local overpass time even later in the afternoon [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528)]. Recent studies have noted systematic radiometric biases between NOAA-21 and SNPP, with NOAA-21 showing a lower bias in some bands and requiring calibration adjustments [[14](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2025.1602145/full), [37](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/19/3557)]. While these biases are manageable, they introduce an additional layer of complexity. Given that merging with NOAA-20 alone is likely to resolve the 11-day gap issue, adding NOAA-21 may provide only marginal improvements in daily sampling frequency without justifying the added preprocessing complexity.

The final two platforms, MODIS/Terra and MODIS/Aqua, represent a fundamentally different class of instrument due to their coarser 1-kilometer spatial resolution [[9](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/vijayveersingh/nasa-firms-active-fire-dataset-modisviirs)]. While MODIS instruments have provided invaluable global fire monitoring data for over two decades, their suitability for this specific classification task is questionable [[48](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/16/3601/2024/)]. The primary obstacle is a severe schema incompatibility. MODIS uses a continuous 0-100% confidence scale, whereas VIIRS employs a discrete categorical system (`low`, `nominal`, `high`) [[7](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/tools/firms/active-fire-data-attributes-modis-viirs), [8](https://firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/descriptions/FIRMS_MODIS_Firehotspots.html), [53](https://forum.earthdata.nasa.gov/viewtopic.php?t=5182)]. This fundamental difference in representing the same concept creates a major hurdle for feature engineering and model input consistency. Furthermore, the 1km resolution means MODIS may fail to detect smaller industrial or agricultural burns that VIIRS captures at 375m, potentially leading to inconsistent ground truth labels between datasets [[45](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid), [46](https://www.facebook.com/IEEE.GRSS/posts/-explore-the-sentinel-2-wildfire-change-detection-s2-wcd-dataset-a-high-resoluti/707119511966020/)]. Although MODIS provides an unparalleled historical record, the benefits of this temporal depth are negated by the resolution mismatch and schema conflict, which threaten the core requirement of consistent feature representation. The ongoing scientific transition from MODIS to VIIRS further underscores that MODIS is a legacy platform, not the future standard for high-fidelity fire detection [[11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0034425723005151)].

## The Viability and Implementation of Multi-VIIRS Sensor Merging

Given that the core problem is a lack of coverage continuity, merging data from multiple VIIRS sensors presents a theoretically sound and practically effective solution. The feasibility of this approach hinges on two critical factors: its proven ability to reduce data gaps and the manageability of any resulting inconsistencies, particularly in radiometric calibration. An examination of NASA documentation and peer-reviewed studies confirms that merging VIIRS data is not merely plausible but is a well-established practice for creating seamless, high-quality climate and environmental records [[12](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/69642/noaa_69642_DS1.pdf), [13](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377108566_Continuity_between_NASA_MODIS_Collection_61_and_VIIRS_Collection_2_products)]. The primary benefit is a dramatic increase in temporal sampling frequency. With three satellites in a near-tandem orbit—SNPP, followed 50 minutes later by NOAA-20, which is itself followed by NOAA-21—the combined constellation provides near-daily coverage of any given point on Earth [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528), [16](https://www.star.nesdis.noaa.gov/jpss/documents/Monthly/2024/JPSS_Monthly_AMP_STAR_202407.pdf)]. This configuration is explicitly designed to maximize observational opportunities. Studies focusing on NPP-VIIRS data have highlighted the existence of substantial and irregular spatial gaps, particularly in certain seasons and hemispheres, demonstrating that reliance on a single satellite is inherently risky [[18](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/18/3449/2026/)]. While the Indian subcontinent is generally well-covered, the user's own observation of an 11-day gap in SNPP data is empirical proof of this vulnerability. By incorporating NOAA-20, which has a slightly offset orbital track, these gaps can be filled, ensuring the daily coverage necessary for the `persistence_90d` feature.

However, the successful implementation of a merged dataset depends on resolving potential data quality issues, chief among them being inter-sensor calibration differences. Research utilizing NASA SIPS Collection 2 Level-1B reflectance products has uncovered systematic radiometric biases between the three VIIRS instruments [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528)]. Specifically, the reflectance values from NOAA-20 and NOAA-21 are consistently lower than those from SNPP. In Visible (VIS) and Near-Infrared (NIR) bands, this offset is approximately 8%, while in Short-Wave Infrared (SWIR) bands, the differences are larger and more complex, with initial offsets as high as 24% before calibration adjustments were applied [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528)]. The underlying cause is attributed to inaccuracies in pre-launch measurements of the solar diffuser's bi-directional reflectance factor (BRF), which propagates as a fixed offset into the post-launch data products [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528)]. These are not random errors but systematic biases that could mislead a machine learning model if left unaddressed. A model might learn to associate a particular hotspot brightness with a specific satellite, mistaking a calibration artifact for a genuine physical signal difference.

This calibration challenge dictates the most efficient engineering strategy under hackathon constraints. Manually developing and validating a complex, per-sensor calibration correction function before the September 4th deadline would be a significant undertaking with uncertain results. A far more pragmatic and robust approach, aligned with modern machine learning best practices, is to treat the satellite platform as a feature within the model itself. XGBoost, as a tree-based ensemble method, is exceptionally adept at handling categorical features and learning complex, non-linear interactions between them and numerical features [[52](https://modis.gsfc.nasa.gov/sci_team/pubs/abstract_new.php?id=67451), [55](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/382153/2/382153.pdf)]. By including a `satellite` column (e.g., "SNPP", "NOAA-20") alongside numerical features like `bright_ti4` and `frp`, the model can implicitly learn to adjust its predictions based on the sensor of origin. This allows the model to account for the systematic offset without requiring explicit data pre-processing. This strategy transforms a difficult calibration problem into a manageable modeling task. The documented radiometric stability of each individual sensor over its mission lifetime provides confidence that while an initial offset exists, it does not introduce catastrophic drift that would undermine this approach [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528), [19](https://www.mdpi.com/journal/remotesensing/special_issues/VIIRS)]. Therefore, the implementation cost of a VIIRS merge is low; it involves simple data concatenation and the addition of one categorical feature, yielding a high return in terms of improved data continuity and model robustness.

## Evaluating the Feasibility of MODIS+VIIRS Fusion

While the VIIRS family offers a straightforward path to enhanced coverage through merging, the question remains whether integrating MODIS data could provide additional value. The arguments for fusion often center on MODIS's much longer historical record, dating back to the year 2000 for Terra and 2002 for Aqua, and its complementary morning and afternoon overpass times [[31](https://firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/download/)]. Theoretically, this vast archive could be used to train a more robust model, and the dual overpass could provide a richer temporal profile of diurnal activity patterns. However, a deeper analysis reveals that the practical challenges of MODIS+VIIRS fusion are substantial and likely outweigh any potential benefits for this specific classification task, especially under the strict time constraints of a hackathon. The primary obstacles are the fundamental incompatibilities in spatial resolution and data schema, which threaten the project's core requirement of consistent feature representation.

The most significant barrier is the resolution mismatch. MODIS provides active fire detections at a 1-kilometer pixel size, whereas VIIRS provides them at a much finer 375-meter resolution [[9](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/vijayveersingh/nasa-firms-active-fire-dataset-modisviirs), [45](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid)]. This difference is not trivial; it fundamentally alters the type of information captured. MODIS pixels are large enough to contain multiple hotspots or to average out the signal from a small, intense fire, potentially failing to detect it altogether [[46](https://www.facebook.com/IEEE.GRSS/posts/-explore-the-sentinel-2-wildfire-change-detection-s2-wcd-dataset-a-high-resoluti/707119511966020/)]. In contrast, VIIRS's higher resolution allows it to pinpoint smaller fires, such as those from agricultural burning or small-scale industrial processes, with greater accuracy [[6](https://pyronear.org/pyro-risks/overview/datasets/NASA-FIRMS_ACTIVE-FIRE_VIIRS.html)]. Attempting to fuse these datasets would require a complex spatial re-gridding process to align the 1km MODIS pixels with the 375m VIIRS pixels. This process would inevitably introduce uncertainty and could corrupt the precise geolocation and intensity information that VIIRS provides. Given that the classification task aims to distinguish between different types of thermal sources, preserving the fidelity of the original detection is paramount. Introducing MODIS data risks degrading this fidelity, making it a high-risk strategy.

Beyond the spatial mismatch, the incompatibility of the confidence scales presents an equally formidable challenge. The MODIS active fire product uses a continuous confidence value ranging from 0 to 100, which can be interpreted as a percentage likelihood of a fire being present [[7](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/tools/firms/active-fire-data-attributes-modis-viirs), [8](https://firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/descriptions/FIRMS_MODIS_Firehotspots.html)]. Conversely, the VIIRS product uses a categorical confidence level, assigning each detection as either `low`, `nominal`, or `high` [[53](https://forum.earthdata.nasa.gov/viewtopic.php?t=5182)]. These two representations of the same underlying concept cannot be directly merged. One would need to establish a mapping scheme, such as defining a rule where MODIS confidence values below 30 map to `low`, 30-70 to `nominal`, and above 70 to `high`. However, such a hard-coded rule would be arbitrary and likely inaccurate, as the thresholds for "confidence" may differ between the two instruments due to their different designs and algorithms [[34](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/catalog/lancemodis-vj214img-nrt-2)]. This forces a choice between either losing the granular information from the MODIS scale or oversimplifying the rich VIIRS scale, neither of which is desirable for a sophisticated classification model. The effort required to research, implement, and validate such a schema transformation is considerable and falls squarely outside the scope of a rapid hackathon development cycle.

Furthermore, the scientific consensus appears to be shifting away from maintaining parallel MODIS and VIIRS operations toward using VIIRS as the successor instrument. NASA's own Land Discipline Team is actively working to transition land data products from MODIS to VIIRS to ensure data continuity [[11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0034425723005151)]. This indicates that MODIS is viewed as a legacy system whose primary role is now being taken over by the newer, higher-resolution VIIRS technology. While MODIS data remains valuable for long-term trend analysis spanning two decades, its relevance for high-fidelity, near-real-time classification tasks is diminishing [[48](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/16/3601/2024/)]. The decision to invest engineering effort in fusing MODIS data, therefore, represents a strategic choice to work with a transitional, rather than a state-of-the-art, platform. Given the documented complexities and the clear superiority of the VIIRS family in terms of resolution and integration potential, pursuing a MODIS+VIIRS fusion strategy is ill-advised for this project.

## Unified Modeling Strategy: Single Model Versus Per-Satellite Models

Once the decision is made to merge data from multiple satellite sources, the next critical question is how to structure the predictive model. The two primary approaches are to train a single, unified model on the entire consolidated dataset or to train separate models for each satellite platform and then reconcile their predictions at inference time. For the specified task of classifying thermal hotspots using XGBoost, the evidence strongly supports the use of a single, unified model. This approach is not only simpler to implement and maintain but also leverages more data, leading to a more robust and generalizable model, which is crucial under the constrained conditions of a hackathon.

Training a unified model on merged VIIRS data offers several distinct advantages. The most significant benefit is the maximization of the training dataset size. By combining data from SNPP and NOAA-20, the amount of training data is effectively doubled. In machine learning, more data generally leads to better model performance, as it allows the algorithm to learn the underlying patterns in the data more thoroughly and reduces the risk of overfitting to noise or idiosyncrasies present in a smaller, single-source dataset. XGBoost, in particular, excels at leveraging large, heterogeneous datasets to capture complex interactions between features [[55](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/382153/2/382153.pdf)]. The proposed strategy of including the `satellite` as a categorical feature is a powerful technique to handle platform-specific variations. As previously discussed, this allows the model to learn and compensate for any systematic calibration biases between SNPP and NOAA-20 [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528)]. Each split in the decision trees within the XGBoost ensemble can condition its logic on the satellite source, effectively creating specialized pathways for interpreting features from each platform without sacrificing the shared knowledge gained from the combined data pool. This hybrid approach achieves the best of both worlds: the robustness of a large dataset and the adaptability to platform-specific characteristics.

In contrast, training separate models for each satellite introduces a host of disadvantages that make it a less suitable choice for this hackathon project. While it ensures that each model is trained on perfectly homogeneous data, thereby eliminating any concern about cross-platform bias, it comes at the cost of drastically reducing the amount of training data available to each individual model. With half the data, each model will have higher variance and will be more susceptible to overfitting. This could lead to poorer generalization performance, especially on the less common classes. More critically, this approach adds significant complexity to the inference pipeline. At prediction time, the system must first determine the satellite source of the incoming hotspot data and then route it to the correct corresponding model. This adds an extra layer of logic and a potential point of failure to the application. Maintaining two separate models also doubles the maintenance overhead. If a new feature needs to be engineered or a bug needs to be fixed, the change must be implemented and validated twice. This increased complexity is disproportionate to the marginal potential gain in accuracy, which is unlikely to be significant given that the VIIRS instruments are highly similar and the primary issue is a systematic offset that can be handled within a single model.

The preference for a unified modeling approach is a standard practice in industry and research when dealing with heterogeneous data sources. It is the default strategy for handling data from multiple sensors or locations because it is scalable, maintainable, and statistically more powerful. The success of this approach relies on the chosen model's ability to handle categorical features effectively, a capability that XGBoost possesses natively. Peer-reviewed studies frequently employ single models trained on multi-source data, demonstrating its viability and effectiveness [[44](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/forests-and-global-change/articles/10.3389/ffgc.2025.1705341/full), [52](https://modis.gsfc.nasa.gov/sci_team/pubs/abstract_new.php?id=67451)]. For a project with a limited timeline and a single ML engineer, the unified model approach is the most efficient use of resources. It focuses engineering effort on building the best possible model with the largest possible dataset, rather than on managing a complex, multi-model infrastructure. The simplicity and power of this strategy make it the unequivocally superior choice for the Smart India Hackathon 2026.

## Final Recommendation: A Pragmatic Path Forward Under Hackathon Constraints

Based on a comprehensive analysis of the satellite platforms, the technical challenges of data fusion, and the practical realities of a hackathon environment, the optimal strategy is clear. The project should abandon the SNPP-only dataset due to its demonstrable 11-day coverage gap, which directly compromises the `persistence_90d` feature. Instead, the recommended course of action is to construct a unified training dataset by merging VIIRS/SNPP and VIIRS/NOAA-20 data for the entire evaluation period from August 2025 to August 2026. This recommendation is grounded in its ability to solve the primary data continuity problem with minimal engineering overhead, thereby maximizing the probability of success within the tight project timeline.

This VIIRS-only merging strategy directly addresses the core requirements of the task. First, it resolves the critical issue of data gaps. The near-tandem orbit of NOAA-20, flying 50 minutes behind SNPP, provides a second daily opportunity to observe thermal events over India, effectively doubling the chances of capturing a hotspot on any given day and ensuring the continuous daily coverage needed for the `persistence_90d` calculation [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528)]. Second, it preserves feature fidelity. All three VIIRS instruments share the same 375-meter spatial resolution and core algorithmic design, meaning that merging them does not degrade the quality or granularity of the input data [[6](https://pyronear.org/pyro-risks/overview/datasets/NASA-FIRMS_ACTIVE-FIRE_VIIRS.html), [34](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/catalog/lancemodis-vj214img-nrt-2)]. The only variable introduced is the satellite platform itself, which is a manageable feature. Third, it avoids the significant pitfalls of MODIS fusion. The incompatibility between MODIS's 1km resolution and VIIRS's 375m resolution, along with the fundamental mismatch in their confidence scales (0-100 integer vs. categorical), introduces a level of complexity and data integrity risk that is entirely unnecessary and counterproductive for this classification task [[7](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/tools/firms/active-fire-data-attributes-modis-viirs), [9](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/vijayveersingh/nasa-firms-active-fire-dataset-modisviirs), [53](https://forum.earthdata.nasa.gov/viewtopic.php?t=5182)].

The implementation of this strategy is straightforward and aligns perfectly with hackathon constraints. The engineering effort consists primarily of a simple data concatenation of the SNPP and NOAA-20 datasets. The main analytical challenge is managing the known radiometric biases between the sensors. Rather than undertaking the complex and time-consuming task of manual pre-processing and calibration correction, the recommended approach is to leverage the capabilities of the XGBoost model. By including the `satellite` (e.g., "SNPP", "NOAA-20") as a native categorical feature, the model can learn to account for the systematic calibration offsets inherent in the raw `bright_ti4` and FRP values [[10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/16/14/2528)]. This is a robust, model-level solution that is far more efficient than attempting to develop and validate a custom calibration function under pressure. Finally, the use of a single, unified XGBoost model trained on the merged data is the correct architectural choice. It utilizes the maximum possible amount of training data to build a more robust and accurate classifier, and it simplifies the inference pipeline by requiring only one model to be loaded and managed [[52](https://modis.gsfc.nasa.gov/sci_team/pubs/abstract_new.php?id=67451), [55](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/382153/2/382153.pdf)].

In summary, the path forward is to pivot from the fragile SNPP-only dataset to a resilient, merged VIIRS/SNPP + NOAA-20 dataset. This decision directly mitigates the primary technical risk to the project—the data gap—while staying within the bounds of a feasible engineering effort. It adheres to the principle of using the simplest solution that effectively solves the problem, avoiding the unnecessary complexity of MODIS fusion and the potential fragility of a per-satellite modeling approach. This pragmatic strategy provides the strongest foundation for building a successful classification pipeline for the Smart India Hackathon 2026.
